How to use your frost date

The average last spring frost is a long-run average, not a switch that makes the garden safe. A cold night can come later. Use it to count backward and forward, then watch the forecast in the week you plant.

One public lookup is the Old Farmer’s Almanac frost-date tool: almanac.com/gardening/frostdates. Type a place and read the spring date. The USDA plant hardiness map at planthardiness.ars.usda.gov shows a zone, which is not the same thing as a frost date.

Colorado State University Extension groups vegetables by how much cold they take:

  • Hardy crops, such as peas, spinach, lettuce, and radishes, can go out as early as 2 to 4 weeks before that average date.
  • Semi-hardy crops, such as beets, carrots, and chard, as early as 0 to 2 weeks before.
  • Tender crops, such as beans, cucumbers, and summer squash, are sown around the frost date. Cucumber and squash transplants wait until frost risk has passed.
  • Very tender crops, such as tomatoes, peppers, and eggplant, typically wait 2 or more weeks after the frost date.

CSU and the University of Minnesota both say soil temperature is a better planting signal than the calendar alone. CSU measures it at 8 a.m., about 4 inches deep, and lists a minimum for each crop in GardenNotes #720. A week of cool days can still stall tomatoes and peppers even after the frost date.

Bloom & Bed Garden turns those windows into dates from the day you type. A range on the calendar means the guide gave a range. It does not know your yard’s soil.

The planting calendar by zone can fill that day from a ZIP code. The date is a NOAA 1991–2020 station normal, not a forecast for this spring.
